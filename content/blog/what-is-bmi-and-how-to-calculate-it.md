## What is BMI and How Do You Calculate It?

BMI — or Body Mass Index — is one of the most widely used tools in medicine and public health for assessing whether a person's body weight is appropriate for their height. It is a simple numerical calculation that takes just two measurements: your weight and your height. Despite being over 200 years old as a concept, it remains the first-line screening tool used by doctors worldwide.

This guide explains exactly what BMI is, how to calculate it, what the numbers mean, and its important limitations.

## What is BMI?

BMI stands for Body Mass Index. It is a measure of body fat based on height and weight that applies to adult men and women. The World Health Organization (WHO) uses BMI as a standard classification system for underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obesity in adults.

It was originally developed by Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet in the 1830s (it was called the Quetelet Index) and later adopted by the medical community as a practical screening tool in the 20th century.

## The BMI Formula

For metric units (kilograms and meters):

BMI = Weight (kg) ÷ Height² (m²)

For imperial units (pounds and inches):

BMI = (Weight (lbs) ÷ Height² (inches²)) × 703

## Step-by-Step BMI Calculation

### Metric Example (Most Common Internationally)

Suppose you weigh 70 kilograms and your height is 1.75 meters.

Step 1: Square your height: 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625

Step 2: Divide weight by squared height: 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.86

Your BMI is 22.86 — which falls in the Normal Weight category.

### Imperial Example (US System)

You weigh 154 pounds and are 5 feet 9 inches tall.

Step 1: Convert height to inches: 5 × 12 + 9 = 69 inches

Step 2: Square height: 69 × 69 = 4761

Step 3: Divide weight by squared height: 154 ÷ 4761 = 0.03234

Step 4: Multiply by 703: 0.03234 × 703 = 22.73

Your BMI is 22.73 — also Normal Weight.

## BMI Categories (WHO Standard)

The World Health Organization defines the following BMI ranges for adults:

- BMI below 18.5: Underweight
- BMI 18.5–24.9: Normal weight (Healthy)
- BMI 25.0–29.9: Overweight
- BMI 30.0–34.9: Obese (Class I)
- BMI 35.0–39.9: Obese (Class II)
- BMI 40.0 and above: Severely Obese (Class III)

### Adjustments for South Asian and East Asian Populations

Research has shown that South Asians (including Indians, Pakistanis, Bangladeshis) and East Asians tend to have higher health risks at lower BMI values compared to Caucasians. Many health organizations now recommend lower cutoffs for these populations:

- BMI 18.5–22.9: Normal Weight
- BMI 23.0–27.4: Overweight (higher risk)
- BMI 27.5 and above: Obese (high risk)

The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) and WHO both recognize these Asian-specific cutoffs.

## Why Does BMI Matter?

High BMI is associated with increased risk of numerous health conditions:

**Cardiovascular Disease:** People with BMI above 25 have significantly higher rates of hypertension, coronary artery disease, and stroke.

**Type 2 Diabetes:** Excess body fat — particularly abdominal fat — is one of the strongest risk factors for insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

**Joint Problems:** Excess weight puts additional mechanical stress on the knees, hips, and spine, accelerating osteoarthritis.

**Sleep Apnea:** Higher BMI is strongly associated with obstructive sleep apnea.

**Certain Cancers:** Obesity is linked to increased risk of colorectal, breast, endometrial, and kidney cancers.

**Low BMI Risks:** Being underweight (BMI below 18.5) is also associated with weakened immune function, osteoporosis, anaemia, and fertility issues.

## The Limitations of BMI

BMI is a useful screening tool but has important limitations that every person should understand:

### 1. BMI Does Not Measure Body Fat Directly
BMI only uses height and weight. It cannot distinguish between fat mass and muscle mass. A muscular athlete may have a BMI in the "overweight" range despite having very little body fat.

### 2. BMI Ignores Fat Distribution
Where fat is stored matters enormously for health. Visceral fat (around the abdomen) is far more dangerous than subcutaneous fat (under the skin elsewhere). Two people can have the same BMI — one with mostly abdominal fat, one with mostly fat on the hips and thighs — with very different health risks.

### 3. BMI Does Not Account for Age
Older adults naturally lose muscle mass and may gain fat while their weight (and BMI) remains stable. This means older adults can appear to have "normal" BMI while actually having a problematic body composition.

### 4. BMI Does Not Account for Gender
Women naturally carry more body fat than men at the same BMI. A 25 BMI in a woman represents a different body composition than a 25 BMI in a man.

### 5. BMI Varies by Ethnicity
As noted above, South and East Asian populations have higher metabolic risk at lower BMI values than European populations.

## Better Alternatives to BMI

Several supplementary measures can give a more complete picture of health:

**Waist Circumference:** A simple measurement that directly assesses abdominal fat. WHO recommends keeping waist circumference below 80 cm (31.5 inches) for women and 94 cm (37 inches) for men.

**Waist-to-Height Ratio:** Divide your waist circumference by your height. A ratio below 0.5 is generally considered healthy. This measure accounts for body frame differently from BMI.

**Body Fat Percentage:** Measured through DEXA scans, hydrostatic weighing, or bioelectrical impedance. This directly measures body fat rather than inferring it from weight and height.

**Waist-to-Hip Ratio:** Indicates how fat is distributed. A ratio above 0.9 (men) or 0.85 (women) indicates abdominal obesity and higher cardiovascular risk.

## What is a Healthy BMI?

For most adults (non-elderly, non-athletic), a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 is considered the healthy range. However, what truly matters is your overall metabolic health — blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, and physical fitness — not any single number.

A person with BMI of 26 who exercises regularly, eats well, and has normal blood tests is clinically healthier than someone with BMI of 22 who is sedentary with prediabetes.

## Calculating BMI for Children and Teenagers

BMI for children and adolescents (aged 2–19) is calculated the same way as for adults, but it is interpreted differently using age- and sex-specific percentile charts rather than fixed cutoffs.

- Below 5th percentile: Underweight
- 5th to 84th percentile: Healthy Weight
- 85th to 94th percentile: Overweight
- 95th percentile and above: Obese

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is a healthy BMI for an Indian adult?**
A: Using Asian-specific cutoffs, a BMI of 18.5–22.9 is normal, 23.0–27.4 is overweight, and above 27.5 is obese for South Asians.

**Q: Can I have a high BMI and still be healthy?**
A: Yes, particularly if you have high muscle mass (athletes, bodybuilders) or a larger bone structure. BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis.

**Q: Is BMI accurate for women?**
A: It is a reasonable screening tool, but remember women naturally carry more body fat than men at the same BMI. Body fat percentage and waist circumference provide more complete information.

**Q: How can I lower my BMI?**
A: A combination of caloric deficit (eating less than you expend) and exercise (both cardio and strength training) is the evidence-based approach. Aim for 0.5 to 1 kg weight loss per week for sustainable results.
